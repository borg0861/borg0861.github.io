Creating a new ancestry for Draw Steel can seem intimidating.This guide is meant to simply the process. Ancestries in Draw Steel are composed of two distinct sections: <a href="#the-basics">The Basics</a> and <a href="#traits">Traits</a>.

### The Basics
The basics of an ancestry cover important details in the fiction, which will help to inform choices when selecting and building Traits. There are 8 elements for basic character information, including:
1. **Name:** What is this ancestry called?
1. **Height Range:** How tall are average individuals belonging to this ancestry?
1. **Weight Range:** How heavy are average individuals belonging to this ancestry?
1. **Life Expectancy:** What is the average lifespan for individuals belonging to this ancestry?
1. **Language(s):** Which language or languages originate with this ancestry?
1. **Size:** What size ranking should be used for this ancestry?
1. **Basic Description:** A basic 3rd-person omnicient description of the ancestry's general appearance, key cultural traits, or other noteable aspects belonging to the ancestry.
1. **Fiction:** A piece of in-world ficiton featuring an "Icon" character who represents that ancestry for players. This fiction should give players a feeling for what it might be like to embody a character of this ancestry.

#### The Basics: Best Practices
Draw Steel is just dripping with flavor. As much as a generic ancestry doesn't make anyone dislike them, generic ancestries are also **boring**. Be sure to add a twist to ancestries you create. Give them some character. The most important thing is that it should make **you** feel excited to play as a character from this ancestry. Something you are passionate about will always come out better.

#### The Basics: Common Pitfalls
When creating ancestry basics, it is easy to fall into a few common traps. Avoid pigeonholing any ancestry into a specific class or type of class. Be sure to give ancestries room to move around and fill all of the possible roles available to them in Draw Steel.

### Traits
Traits are the mechanical underpinnings of each ancestry. Using an overall Ancestry Points budget, each ancestry can easily maintain a general balance. Within this seciton, there are three elements. They include,
1. **Signature Trait(s):** Signature Traits are those traits, abilities, or features which are common to all members of an ancestry. These are almost always biological aspects belonging to a species, rather than features or traits which could arise from culture or upbringing. The majority of signature traits cost 1 point, though a few (like the Revenant’s “Tough but Withered”) are worth 2.
1. **Disposable Ancestry Points:** Disposable Ancestry Points are the points which a player can spend to further customize their character's ancestral heritage. The difference between the Overall Ancestry Point budget, and the points spent on Signature Traits represents the available Disposable Ancestry Points players may use on Purchased Traits. This value should generally not be lower than 2.
1. **Purchased Traits:** Purchased Traits are the traits on which the player spends their Disposable Ancestry Points. The number of purchased traits available to ancestries can vary widely. At minimum, each ancestry should have a selection of three 1-cost traits and two 2-cost traits. This gives a good balance of different combinations, while not overwhelming the player with options. In the rare case that an ancestry has 4 Disposable Ancestry Points, the recommended minimum number of traits becomes four 1-cost traits and three 2-cost traits. Additional options can always be added, but should rarely exceed three above the recommended minimum. It is wise to keep the options easy to grasp for a player.
1. **Quick Build:** A selection of purchased traits which players can use to quickly build a character of this ancestry.

#### Overall Ancestry Points
Ancestry Points are an abstract measure of the balance of abilities granted by an ancestry. Each ancestry begins with 4 Ancestry Points which can be used to purchase Signature Traits or passed on to the player to spend on Purchased Traits.

#### Quick Build
A suggested quick build for your ancestry should include a set of traits that meets three criteria. First, quick build traits should be representative of the ancestry's core idea. Second, quick build traits should be broadly applicable to any class. Finally, quick build traits should be mechanically simple. No extra abilities, no triggers to keep track of, just passive abilities. 

In the case that an ancestry does not have enough simple options to meet the third criteria. In that case, the quick build should include traits which are only applicable on the hero’s turn, such as a signature action.

#### Trait Listing
The following suggested traits can be found in Draw Steel, and are categorized by how much each trait costs.
- **-1 Point**
    - **Size `1S`.** An ancestry smaller than the norm grants an additional point. This also applies if the ancestry's effective size is `1S`, as with the Memonek's "Lightweight" Signature Trait.
- **1 Point**
    - **Size `1L`.** An ancestry one-size larger than the norm costs an additional point.
    - **An edge applied to two overlapping skills.** The High Elf’s “High Elf Glamour” gives an edge on tests using the flirt or persuade skills. There will be many situations where both skills could apply. Similarly, the Wode Elf’s “Wode Elf Glamour” gives an edge to hide and sneak. These skills will also see use in many of the same situations. It’s also worth noting the Human’s “Perseverance”, which both gives an edge on endurance tests, and include another minor benefit of having speed 1 higher when slowed. <mark class="editorial-highlight">An edge on skills can be a pretty boring effect. It doesn’t give the player new tools, or change how they approach a problem.</mark>
    - **+1 speed, +1 stability, +1 disengage, or +3 stamina per echelon.** All of these bonuses are the same ones granted by many kits. These are basic mechanical bonuses which are easy to include. 
    - **A characteristic counts as 1 higher when resisting potencies.** Though this bonus is only granted for Might on MCDM ancestries, there is little reason it could not be applied to other abilities. <mark class="editorial-highlight">Might is the most frequently targeted characteristic, with Agility close behind. If a trait increases Reason, Intuition, or Presence, it would likely be balanced add on a minor effect to reflect that the primary bonus will see little use.</mark>
    - **Basic ribbon ability.** Ribbon abilities are any ability, feature, or trait not directly useful in combat. There are many of these, such as the Orc’s “Passionate Artisan” or Memonek’s “Systemic Mind”. These are the most difficult to balance, since they tend to be unique to the fantasy of that ancestry.
    - **Triggered action.** Triggered actions granted by ancestries are generally weaker than those granted by a class, though not by much. An effect halving damage or dealing `1d10 + Level` damage are the simplest to include, but any class triggered action could be modified to result in an effective ancestry triggered action.
- **2 Points**
    - **Immunity to a condition.** Immunity to Weakened, Slowed, Dazed, Frightened, and Bleeding are all represented in abilities given by core ancestries. Immunity to Grabbed or Restrained would also be roughly equivalent. <mark class="editorial-highlight">Notably only immunity to one of the two, even if a trait giving immunity to both makes a lot of narrative sense.</mark>
    - **+2 speed or +6 stamina per echelon.** These two bonuses from kits are also available, with a higher cost commensurate with the higher bonus.
    - **Signature Action.** Ancestries can offer Signature Actions such as the Wode Elf’s “The Wode Defends” or the Dragon Knight’s “Draconic Pride”. <mark class="editorial-highlight">Notably both of the examples we shared seem to be equal in power to standard signature abilities, if not slightly stronger.</mark>
    - **Maneuver.** Ancestries can also offer maneuvers. Notable examples include the Humans’ “Determination” and Time Raider “Minor Acceleration.” <mark class="editorial-highlight">If an effect has little combat utility, it might be better to price it as a 1 cost ribbon ability, even if it is technically a maneuver.</mark>

#### Traits: Best Practices
When creating new traits, be certain to integrate both fiction and mechanical benefits. Every trait should be a window into the background and identity of an ancestry, while also providing meaningful bonuses.

Be certain that each ancestry has a mixture of flavorful banner traits, and highly impactful mechanichal traits.

#### Traits: Common Pitfalls
Similar to the pitfalls discussed in The Basics, it is important to avoid pigeonholing a single ancestry into an one class or type of class. Avoid creating a Signature Trait which increases the range of gravitic abilities, which would then be useless when playing a Tech-based character. Purchased Traits can often be more niche, since players not fitting that niche can purchase different abilities.

It is also wise to avoid ribbon traits costing 2 points. Draw Steel is a game about fighting monsters, and most ancestry traits provide support to that core theme.

Avoid abilities that hurt or hinder allies. One of the core designs of Draw Steel is being heroic, and part of that is not accidentally blasting an ally with an AoE spell. If you have an ability that creates difficult terrain, be certain it only applies to enemies, even at the risk it might not make sense with the fiction.

Additionally, avoid any negative effect which negates a different positive effect and thus results in a boring, net neutral character. Players should be unable to accidentally hamstring themselves by picking a potentially punishing downside they then cannot deal with. <mark class="editorial-highlight">Reduced speed is a key example of a poorly chosen negative trait.</mark>

## When to Break the Rules
As you become more familiar with buildign ancestries and the balance of Draw Steel, you can begin to ignore one or more of the rules and metrics outlined in this guide. As a general rule for rule breaking, only break a rule once you understand why it exists, and then only break one rule at a time. Of course, that itself is a rule that should sometimes be broken…
